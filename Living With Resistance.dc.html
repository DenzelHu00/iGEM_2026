Some of the hardest parts of an infection aren’t medical.

We’re a student team at the University of British Columbia listening to people living with antibiotic‑resistant infections, and to the families, caregivers and clinicians around them. The science is well studied. What it does to a life is not. That’s the part we’re asking about.

First, in plain language

No background needed. If you already know all of this, skip ahead.

What resistance means

Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria. In any group of bacteria, a few already survive a given drug. Antibiotics clear out the rest, and the survivors are what’s left to multiply. That’s resistance: not a bug that turned nasty because of something you did, and not a punishment for taking medicine you needed.

It usually still works

Almost all of the time, antibiotics do their job, partly because doctors keep other options in reserve for when the first one fails. Antibiotics are arguably the most important medicine ever made; without them there would be no transplants, no routine surgery. Resistance is what makes protecting them urgent, not a reason to fear them.

When it doesn’t

An infection that should clear in a week drags on for months, needs harsher drugs, or keeps coming back. That means more appointments, more waiting, more time away from work and people, and sometimes an isolation room, or being asked to keep your distance from your own family.

And “psychosocial”?

A clunky word for an ordinary thing: how an illness lands on your mood, your relationships, your work and your sense of yourself. Hospitals measure the infection closely. Far fewer people ask about the rest of it.

A note on words. You may have heard “antibiotic resistance” or “superbug”. Researchers say antimicrobial resistance, or AMR, because it covers more than bacteria and more than one mechanism. We use that term here, but use whichever words feel natural when you talk to us.

The parts we want to hear about

Every one of these came out of interviews other researchers have published with patients, caregivers, nurses and doctors. If something here is familiar, or if the thing that mattered most to you isn’t on the list at all, that’s exactly what we’d like to know.

  • Feeling contaminated
    Patients in these studies used words like dirty and ashamed. Gowns and gloves protect people, and they also send a message.
  • Visitors who don’t come
    Staff have described family and friends staying away from isolated patients because they were frightened of catching something.
  • Nobody explained it
    Some patients had never heard the term for their own condition after years of treatment. Not knowing makes everything else heavier.
  • No one to compare notes with
    Other long illnesses have communities and support groups. Resistant infections mostly don’t, so people carry it privately.
  • What it costs to keep going
    Repeat appointments, travel from out of town, unpaid days, prescriptions. For some people the barrier is simply getting back to a doctor.
  • Not knowing if it’s over
    Whether it comes back, whether the next drug works, whether you’re still carrying something. That uncertainty is its own long illness.

This isn’t only patients. Nurses and doctors interviewed in the same research described frustration, guilt and helplessness of their own, caught between the precautions that keep a ward safe and what those precautions do to the person inside the room. Caregivers rarely get asked about any of it. If that’s you, we want to hear from you too.

Who’s asking

We’re the Human Practices subteam of iGEM UBC: six undergraduates working alongside a synthetic biology project on antimicrobial resistance. Our job on the team is the human half: making sure what gets built in the lab answers something people actually need.

We are students, not clinicians. We can’t give medical advice or comment on your care, but we can listen carefully, and we can carry what we hear into work that clinicians and researchers will read.

There are four ways to take part, and one of them takes ten minutes

A short anonymous survey, a one-to-one conversation, a written story in your own words, or nothing at all for now. Just tell us you’re interested and we’ll keep you posted. You can stop at any point, and you never have to answer a question you’d rather not.

What we’re doing, and why

Three stages, in order. The literature search is finished; we’re at the start of the second one, which is where you come in.

01

The problem

Antimicrobial resistance is usually counted: prescriptions written, resistant samples, days in hospital, dollars. British Columbia has some of the best of that data anywhere: nineteen years of it, fifty-one million prescriptions, a stewardship programme that cut antibiotic use in small children by more than half. It is genuinely good work, and it describes an organism rather than a person.

We spent this spring reading everything we could find on the other side of it, and three things stood out. Most of what exists comes from hospital samples, which systematically misses the people least likely to end up in a hospital record. The studies that do ask patients directly are small, scattered across different countries and different infections, and almost none of them are from BC. And the same barriers keep surfacing in all of them anyway: shame, isolation, silence, cost, and the plain fact that nobody explained the diagnosis.

The burden also falls unevenly. Our review kept landing on the same groups: people in long-term care, people experiencing homelessness, refugees, Indigenous communities living with underfunded infrastructure, cancer patients, newborns, and the healthcare workers alongside them. Resistance follows the fault lines that were already there. That makes it an equity problem, not just a microbiology one.

02

What we’re doing

The reading is done, and we’ve been learning from people who know this from the inside. Dr. Bob Hancock, Canada Research Chair in Health and Genomics at UBC, walked us through the science and where public understanding of it falls short. Dr. Richard Lester, an infectious disease physician, showed us how this looks from the clinic. And Sunny Loo, who lives with vasculitis and long-term antibiotic dependency and is a patient partner on the BC Antimicrobial Stewardship Program, has been shaping what we ask, and how we ask it. Now we’re going to the people the papers are about.

  • Ask the people it happened to. Surveys, one-to-one interviews and written accounts, from patients, from the family and friends who looked after them, and from the nurses and physicians who treat these infections in BC.
  • Name the barriers, in their words. Turn what we hear into an honest map of the psychosocial barriers people actually run into, starting from what exists in each community rather than what it lacks.
  • Fix the explaining problem. With UBC Geering Up we’re building AMR teaching for school-age students, and we publish patient and clinician interviews on our social channels, because “nobody told me” came up too often to ignore.
  • Take it back to the bench. Use the map to steer our own team’s laboratory work, and publish the whole thing in plain language, free to read, for patients as well as researchers.
03

What we hope changes

That a patient with a resistant infection gets asked how they’re coping, not only how the wound looks. That nobody spends a week in an isolation room without being told why, in words they can use with their own family. That BC has patient-side evidence at all, so the next strategy written here can be built for the people it lands hardest on, rather than around them. And that the next team of students designing something for AMR starts by reading what patients said, because it exists and it’s easy to find.

We’re one undergraduate team with one season. We’re not going to fix this. We can make the human side of it harder to overlook.

Stage two only works if people talk to us

Everything above depends on hearing from people who have lived it. If you have, ten minutes of your time changes what we’re able to say.
